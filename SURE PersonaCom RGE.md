Recursion Geometry for Structured Reasoning

A Unified Architecture: Concept, Method, Applications, Limitations, and Validation Roadmap

Prepared from the supplied working document. This edition consolidates repeated material and distinguishes the proposed architecture from claims that require independent verification.

Abstract

This document describes a proposed computational architecture called a recursion-geometry engine. Its central idea is to represent formally structured problems through a common set of objects—states, relations, constraints, recursive mappings, discriminants, stability conditions, and admissible regions—and to evaluate those objects through a shared analysis pipeline. The intended applications include mathematical structures, computational systems, dynamical models, and selected areas of theoretical physics.

The architecture is presented as a cross-domain consistency and inference framework, rather than as a single physical theory. In principle, a common representation could make it easier to compare assumptions, detect incompatible constraints, identify invalid regions, and trace the consequences of recursive rules across otherwise separate domains. The working document describes modules for cosmology, neutrino physics, gravity, dark matter, and gauge-anomaly checks.

The central proposal is promising only to the extent that it can be made precise and tested. Terms such as “universal,” “resolution,” “stability,” and “structural unification” require explicit definitions. Claims of scientific compatibility, predictive power, expert-level performance, or superiority to existing tools must be supported by reproducible code, mathematical derivations, benchmark comparisons, and independent review. This document therefore separates the architecture’s conceptual proposal from the empirical and formal evidence needed to establish its performance.

1. Executive Overview

The proposed system aims to provide one reusable reasoning architecture for problems that share formal structure. Instead of building a completely separate reasoning process for every field, it maps each problem into a common representation and applies a common sequence of checks.

At a high level, the proposed workflow is:

Represent the problem as a structured domain containing states, variables, relations, assumptions, and constraints.

Specify the recursive mapping or transition rule that governs how the represented state changes or how inferences are extended.

Define discriminants or validity conditions that distinguish admissible from inadmissible states.

Analyze fixed points, convergence, divergence, stability, and relevant domain boundaries where those concepts are mathematically defined.

Check for inconsistent assumptions, incompatible constraints, or invalid transitions.

Return a result with its assumptions, derivation path, validity conditions, and unresolved limitations.

The proposed contribution is not merely that recursion, constraints, or stability analysis exist; each is already used in established areas of mathematics and computing. The distinctive claim is that a specific shared architecture can organize these methods across multiple domains in a useful and verifiable way. Demonstrating that distinct domain modules genuinely use the same core mechanism—rather than only sharing terminology—will be central to evaluating the project.

The system should therefore be described provisionally as a proposed cross-domain structured-inference architecture. Stronger labels, such as a universal solver or a system that performs at or above expert level, should be reserved for a stage when clear definitions and comparative evidence support them.

2. Core Concept: Structured Intelligence

2.1 What counts as a structured problem?

A structured problem is one whose relevant elements can be represented with explicit objects and relationships. Depending on the domain, these may include variables, states, functions, equations, rules, constraints, transition relations, boundary conditions, invariants, or validation criteria.

Examples include a dynamical system with a defined state transition, a geometric object constrained by equations, a program represented as a graph, a logical theory with inference rules, or a physical model with parameters and observational bounds. Not every problem in these fields is fully formalized, and the fact that a problem can be represented does not by itself mean that it can be solved efficiently or uniquely.

2.2 Operational meaning of intelligence

In this project, “structured intelligence” is an operational description of a system intended to perform tasks such as pattern extraction, constraint checking, consistency analysis, stability classification, and inference over formally represented domains. The term does not imply consciousness, human-like understanding, independent goals, or general intelligence.

Whether the system successfully performs these tasks is a matter for testing. It is not enough to state that the architecture detects contradictions or resolves problems; each capability needs a defined input, output, success criterion, and evaluation procedure.

2.3 Proposed unified principle

The proposed unifying idea is recursion geometry: representing a domain in terms of a structured state space, recursive or iterative relations, validity conditions, and stability-related structure. A domain is mapped into this representation, analyzed with shared procedures, and returned with a record of the conditions under which the result holds.

The term “geometry” should be used precisely. If a module uses an actual geometric space, manifold, metric, or geometric transformation, those objects should be defined. If “geometry” is being used more broadly to mean the organization of states and relations, the document and implementation should say so explicitly.

3. Proposed Architecture and Analysis Pipeline

3.1 Domain representation

Each module begins with a formal description of the domain under study. A practical representation may include:

State space: the possible configurations or states under consideration.

Variables and parameters: quantities that define a state or control a model.

Relations and transition rules: equations, mappings, recursive updates, or inference rules.

Constraints: equalities, inequalities, boundary conditions, conservation requirements, or other restrictions.

Discriminants: explicitly defined tests that separate cases or identify a change in admissibility or behavior.

Stability criteria: definitions appropriate to the system, such as fixed-point stability, boundedness, convergence, or robustness.

Evidence and provenance: source datasets, assumptions, units, parameter values, code version, and derivation history.

3.2 Recursive mapping

A recursive mapping describes how a state, representation, or inference step produces a subsequent state or step. In a simple discrete form, it may be written as x_(n+1) = F(x_n; θ), where x_n is the current state, F is the transition rule, and θ denotes parameters. Other domains may require relations rather than functions, continuous dynamics rather than discrete iteration, or proof rules rather than numerical states.

The framework should not force every domain into an unsuitable mathematical form. A valid cross-domain architecture needs a clear interface and explicit translation rules, while allowing domain-specific semantics and calculations where required.

3.3 Discriminants and validity checks

A discriminant is used in the working concept as a condition that helps classify a structure. In mathematics, “discriminant” also has narrower established meanings, such as expressions that characterize repeated roots or changes in algebraic structure. To avoid ambiguity, each implementation should define the object it calls a discriminant and state what it proves or tests.

A validity check might test whether a parameter lies in an allowed interval, whether constraints can be satisfied simultaneously, whether a transition preserves an invariant, or whether a solution falls within a model’s domain of applicability. Such checks can reject a result under stated assumptions; they do not automatically prove that the assumptions are physically correct.

3.4 Stability and state evolution

Stability analysis depends on the kind of system being examined. A fixed point, an attractor, a bounded trajectory, a stable numerical scheme, and a robust solution are different concepts. The engine must select a mathematically appropriate definition for each module rather than treating them as interchangeable.

Where suitable, the analysis may examine fixed points, trajectories, convergence, divergence, sensitivity to initial conditions, parameter changes, or boundary behavior. If a module does not calculate these properties, its output should be described as a heuristic classification or a proposed test—not as a proof of stability.

3.5 Resolution and output

“Resolution” should mean a specific operation, not a general promise that the system makes problems disappear. Depending on the task, a result may be a consistent assignment of parameters, a reduced constraint set, a proof that a model is inconsistent under specified assumptions, a stable region, a counterexample, or a list of unresolved conditions.

A reliable output should include the conclusion, the assumptions used, the checks performed, the relevant calculations or proof steps, the data and code versions, and any failure or uncertainty. When the available information is insufficient, the correct output is an explicit unresolved result.

4. Intended Capabilities

4.1 Pattern and structure extraction

The architecture is intended to identify recurring relations, invariants, constraint patterns, and similarities between formal representations. To substantiate this capability, the project should specify what counts as a pattern, how candidate patterns are generated, and how false matches are rejected.

4.2 Contradiction and constraint analysis

The system is intended to identify incompatible equations, assumptions, parameter ranges, or constraints. A contradiction report should identify the specific set of conditions that cannot all hold together. Where possible, it should provide a minimal inconsistent subset or a counterexample. The system should distinguish a genuine logical contradiction from a failed numerical search, a missing assumption, or a model that is simply underdetermined.

4.3 Limits and admissible regions

The architecture aims to identify where a representation remains valid, where a constraint is violated, and where a model’s assumptions cease to apply. These boundaries must be derived from explicit conditions or supported by a documented computational test.

4.4 Inference and prediction

The project uses “prediction” in a broad structural sense: inferring consequences of a recursive rule or determining how a state may evolve under a model. This is distinct from an empirically validated forecast. Predictive claims require a defined target, a comparison with data or held-out cases, baseline methods, and a measure of error.

4.5 Cross-domain consistency

A shared representation could allow the system to compare the formal assumptions and consequences of different modules. However, consistency within each module does not establish consistency across fields. Cross-domain integration requires explicit mappings between the relevant variables, assumptions, units, and meanings. Similar mathematical form alone is not proof that two physical systems are equivalent.

5. Scope and Functional Limits

The working concept is deliberately narrower than a claim of unrestricted intelligence. It describes a system that operates on representations supplied by people or other software and applies the rules implemented in its architecture. Its useful scope depends on the quality of those representations, the validity of the rules, and the completeness of the tests.

Goal selection: unless a goal-generation mechanism is explicitly implemented, the system evaluates tasks supplied by a user or workflow.

Representation dependence: it cannot reliably analyze aspects of a problem that are omitted, misrepresented, or not expressible in the chosen representation.

Novelty: it may derive results not explicitly listed in its inputs, but that is different from demonstrating open-ended creativity or inventing a new scientific field.

Self-modification: the system should not be described as rewriting its own architecture unless such behavior is implemented and evaluated.

Uncertainty and randomness: these can be modeled formally, but they cannot be handled correctly merely by calling them “structure.”

Human domains: social, ethical, aesthetic, emotional, and personal problems may contain formal structure, but their interpretation often depends on context, values, and evidence beyond a formal constraint engine.

Proof limits: a result is only as strong as its assumptions, algorithms, and verification. A consistent output can still be based on an incorrect model.

These are engineering and epistemic limits, not necessarily permanent impossibilities. Some can be addressed by adding modules, probabilistic methods, human review, richer representations, or formal verification. The document should describe what the current implementation actually does, rather than treating all absent functions as impossible in principle.

6. Application to Theoretical Physics

The working document describes intended modules for cosmology, neutrino physics, gravity, dark matter, and gauge anomalies. These should be treated as separate technical case studies connected by a common architecture. The claims below summarize the supplied draft; they are not independently verified in this edited document.

6.1 Cosmology and the parameter β

The draft reports that a constant recursion parameter β cannot simultaneously satisfy an early-time discriminant constraint and produce the required late-time acceleration. It therefore proposes a scale-dependent parameter β(a), where a is the cosmological scale factor.

The stated interpretation is that the constant-parameter formulation fails and that a running β(a) is a possible extension. To establish this conclusion, the project must publish the governing equations, define the discriminant, show the derivation of the early- and late-time bounds, and compare the resulting expansion history with relevant observations. A scale-dependent parameter is a possible modeling choice, not by itself a resolution of the cosmological problem.

6.2 Neutrino mass constraints

The draft gives a predicted neutrino mass sum of Σmν = 0.06525 eV and compares it with a quoted DESI DR2 bound of Σmν < 0.0642 eV at 95% confidence. It characterizes the difference as small and suggests that a minor adjustment to the lightest mass could remove the tension.

This comparison requires careful verification. A quoted upper limit depends on the dataset combination, cosmological model, statistical assumptions, and publication version. The numerical difference between a model value and an upper limit is not, by itself, a Gaussian “sigma tension.” The project should cite the exact analysis, state the neutrino mass ordering and oscillation inputs, and recompute the comparison using the relevant likelihood or confidence construction.

6.3 Gravity and black-hole observables

The draft reports that curvature corrections and deviations in quasinormal modes and black-hole shadow predictions fall below observational bounds. To substantiate this, the module should specify the underlying metric or field equations, the perturbative approximation, the observable being compared, the units, the parameter range, and the observational analysis from which each bound is drawn.

A result described as a “pass” should include a reproducible calculation and a quantitative margin to the applicable constraint. If the framework merely estimates an order of magnitude, the conclusion should be stated at that level.

6.4 Dark matter and dimensional consistency

The draft gives a candidate dark-matter mass scale near 10⁻¹¹ eV and notes that a Compton-wavelength expression must include the appropriate factors of ħ and c when converting between mass and length units. This is an important type of check for a cross-domain framework: dimensional consistency should be enforced explicitly.

The draft also quotes a length of approximately 6.4 × 10⁻¹⁶ kpc after correction. That value and its interpretation must be recomputed from the stated mass and unit convention before being presented as a result. A mass scale being numerically compatible with a broad class of ultralight candidates does not establish that the candidate satisfies structure-formation, astrophysical, or cosmological constraints.

6.5 Gauge-anomaly checks

The draft states that the framework passes checks for SU(3)²U(1), SU(2)²U(1), U(1)³, gravitational²U(1), and the Witten anomaly. A meaningful verification must provide the field content, representations, hypercharge or charge assignments, chirality conventions, multiplicities, and explicit sums or arguments for each anomaly condition.

If those calculations are correct, the result would establish anomaly cancellation for the specified field content and assumptions. It would not, on its own, establish a universal physical theory or validate unrelated modules.

6.6 Observational integration

The draft names Pantheon+, DESI BAO, and Planck as intended or available observational inputs. A credible data interface should document dataset versions, data provenance, covariance matrices, likelihood definitions, nuisance parameters, preprocessing, and reproducible configuration. Merely providing a place to load datasets does not mean that the system has been observationally validated.

6.7 Physics conclusion

The physics modules offer potential test cases for the architecture because they involve different kinds of constraints and evidence. Their value depends on whether the framework produces correct, reproducible results and makes the reasoning easier to audit. The current draft identifies a cosmological limitation under constant β and proposes β(a) as a research direction; the other stated passes should be treated as provisional until their calculations and sources are supplied.

7. Mathematical Interpretation

7.1 Formal objects

Mathematically, the architecture can be described as an inference procedure over structured spaces. Depending on the module, the formal objects may include sets, functions, relations, graphs, constraint systems, manifolds, recursive mappings, discriminants, and stability conditions.

The framework’s generality would come from a shared interface and a well-defined set of operations—not from assuming that all domains are mathematically identical. Each module should specify its object types and the conditions under which the common operations preserve meaning.

7.2 Discriminants and constraints

A discriminant or validity function may partition a domain into regions that satisfy or violate a criterion. For example, a constraint function g(x) may define an admissible set through g(x) ≥ 0. That example is only a template; the correct formulation depends on the problem. The implementation should identify whether each test is necessary, sufficient, approximate, or heuristic.

7.3 Stability

Stability is not a single universal property. In discrete dynamics, one may study the behavior of iterates under a mapping. In differential equations, stability may concern trajectories near an equilibrium. In numerical computation, it may concern error growth. In constraint systems, one may instead study robustness under perturbation. A shared framework can organize these analyses, but the definitions and proof obligations remain domain-specific.

7.4 Structural correspondence and unification

The draft uses “structural isomorphism” to explain how distinct domains might be unified. An isomorphism requires a defined mapping that preserves the relevant structure and has the appropriate invertibility properties. Where only some features correspond, terms such as “structural analogy,” “shared representation,” or “partial correspondence” may be more accurate.

The project should specify what is preserved when moving between domains: state transitions, constraints, invariants, algebraic relations, or some other property. Without this, “unification” remains a research hypothesis rather than a demonstrated mathematical result.

7.5 Resolution as a formal operation

A rigorous account should define what it means for a structure to be resolved. Possible operations include finding a satisfying assignment, deriving a contradiction, reducing a constraint system, proving a property, or identifying a stable subset. These outcomes are distinct and should be reported separately.

8. Computational Interpretation

From a computer-science perspective, the proposal is a domain-general inference architecture that organizes structured input, recursive transitions, validity tests, stability analysis, and result reporting. Its implementation may share features with graph rewriting, fixed-point iteration, constraint propagation, static analysis, rule-based inference, or formal verification. These comparisons describe neighboring techniques; they do not establish that the system is categorically unlike them.

8.1 Suggested module contract

Each domain module should expose a clear contract:

Input schema: required fields, units, types, assumptions, and optional datasets.

Normalization: checks that inputs are well-formed and consistently represented.

Core mapping: the recursive or relational rule being evaluated.

Validators: named constraints, discriminants, invariants, and stability tests.

Result schema: outcome, derivation, warnings, failed checks, and unresolved questions.

Reproducibility record: software version, parameters, data provenance, and execution configuration.

8.2 Verification and failure modes

The engine should distinguish a proven result from a numerical estimate, a failed search, an unsupported input, or a timeout. It should preserve counterexamples and log failed checks rather than silently discarding them. Where a proof is not available, the output should state what was tested and what remains unproven.

8.3 Comparison with existing tools

The architecture should be compared against appropriate baselines rather than defined only by what it is not. Depending on the task, relevant baselines may include numerical solvers, symbolic computation, SAT/SMT solvers, theorem provers, constraint-programming systems, scientific pipelines, and domain-specific validation tools. A benchmark should show whether the shared architecture improves accuracy, coverage, auditability, speed, reuse, or another measurable outcome.

9. Collaboration and Research Workflow

The proposed architecture is intended to be modular. A collaborator should be able to contribute a formal domain description, constraints, recursive relations, datasets, or a new module without needing to redesign the entire core. This depends on a stable interface, documentation, tests, and transparent assumptions.

9.1 Collaboration process

Define the domain and the question to be evaluated.

Supply equations, rules, parameters, constraints, datasets, and source references.

Map the domain into the shared representation and document any translation assumptions.

Run validation, consistency, and stability checks appropriate to that domain.

Compare outputs with known analytical results, trusted software, published bounds, or held-out data.

Review failures and refine the module without concealing incompatible assumptions.

Publish a reproducible report that states what passed, what failed, and what remains uncertain.

9.2 Existing tools

The draft anticipates integration with Python, NumPy, SciPy, JAX, PyTorch, symbolic systems, numerical solvers, simulation frameworks, and data pipelines. These integrations should be described concretely: which interfaces exist, which are planned, what data formats are supported, and what functionality is delegated to external tools. Compatibility should not be described as complete until it has been implemented and tested.

9.3 Useful collaborator contributions

Formalizing definitions and proof obligations.

Auditing equations, units, assumptions, and parameter choices.

Implementing or testing domain modules.

Providing trusted reference cases and independent benchmarks.

Verifying observational data and statistical methods.

Reviewing software architecture, reproducibility, and documentation.

10. Funding and Project Evaluation

A funding proposal should focus on a specific, testable research plan rather than claiming that the architecture is already universally capable. Its strongest case would be that a shared representation may reduce duplicated validation work and help identify inconsistencies across models, provided the project demonstrates this advantage.

Implement a reproducible core engine and a small set of representative modules.

Validate each module against analytic results, trusted reference implementations, or published benchmarks.

10.2 Deliverables

A test suite with positive, negative, boundary, and adversarial cases.

Reproducible modules for selected physics and formal-system examples.

Benchmark reports comparing the system with relevant existing methods.

10.3 Risks and mitigation

The main risks are not yet established to be low. They include ambiguity in the core terminology, accidental use of different methods under a single label, incorrect domain translations, hidden dependence on domain-specific code, numerical or unit errors, weak benchmark design, and overinterpretation of partial results. These risks can be mitigated through formal definitions, independent review, modular tests, source tracking, dimensional analysis, and explicit comparison with established tools.

The cosmology issue identified in the draft—the failure of a constant β formulation under the stated requirements—should be treated as a research result to document and investigate, not as a minor implementation detail unless the derivation establishes that characterization.

11. Publication Abstract

We describe a proposed cross-domain structured-inference architecture based on a common representation of states, recursive relations, constraints, discriminants, and stability conditions. The aim is to support consistency checking and structured inference across formal domains without requiring every module to share identical domain-specific semantics. The architecture is intended for applications in mathematical and computational systems and selected areas of theoretical physics.

The working implementation is described as including modules for cosmology, neutrino mass constraints, gravitational observables, dark-matter parameter analysis, and gauge-anomaly checks. The draft reports several candidate results, including a limitation of a constant cosmological recursion parameter and the need to investigate a scale-dependent extension. These results require reproducible derivations, verified data references, and independent review before they can be treated as established findings.

The principal research question is whether a shared recursion-geometry representation can improve the auditability, reuse, and cross-domain consistency of structured scientific analyses. We outline a validation strategy based on explicit formal definitions, benchmark comparisons, dimensional checks, source-tracked data integration, and reproducible module-level tests. The architecture’s generality and scientific value are therefore treated as hypotheses to be evaluated, rather than assumed from the breadth of its intended applications.

12. Conclusion

The project’s central idea is to use a shared formal architecture to analyze structured problems through recursive relations, constraints, discriminants, stability criteria, and explicit validation. Its ambition is cross-domain reuse: the same core workflow would support different modules while preserving the mathematical meaning and evidence requirements of each domain.

The most important next step is precision. The architecture needs a formal specification of its data model and operations, followed by reproducible demonstrations on well-defined tasks. Each claimed result should be traceable to equations, assumptions, code, data, and a clear test. Claims of universality, expert-level performance, or scientific unification should follow evidence rather than precede it.

Presented this way, recursion geometry is a research program with a clear direction: formalize the common substrate, define the limits of its operations, test the modules against established methods, and determine whether cross-domain structure yields practical advantages. Its significance will depend on the quality and reproducibility of those demonstrations.
